# R1: the evaluation design

**Question.** How fast rule and query engines are built in Rust (salsa and rust-analyzer, Datalog and dataflow, spreadsheets, cedar, the evaluator's form), and which shape fits Tally's engine under phase 1's strict rules, readable by its owner. Speed is the user's core concern.
**Date:** 2026-10-30. **Agent:** R1, session 8 (engine prep, part A). Paper only: no build, no benchmark, no interpreter run. Every web source was fetched 2026-10-30.

---

## 2. Summary

1. **Where the time goes is structure, not node speed.** shape.md shows refcore stepping 5 477 days 64 times in A (5.4 % non-empty), 16 070 days 8 times in E2 (6.7 %), and a day-keyed recurrence recomputed under 19 projections in X (95 % of 2.27 M misses). An event-driven walk, prefix sharing between timelines and a context trimmed to what an item reads remove that work outright; a faster evaluator only makes it cheaper. The order of magnitude is in the walk and the memo key (shape.md, "What carries over" 3; **inference** for the multiplier).
2. **Evaluator form: tree-walking over a resolved tree.** Published Rust measurements give closures about 1.5× over a plain AST walk and bytecode no better than closures (Mitchell 2020: 2.1 s, 1.4 s, 1.5 s); Larose et al. (OOPSLA 2023) found AST and bytecode interpreters on par. cedar, IronCalc and refcore all walk trees. Tally's expressions are at most 8 deep (`LOAD-DEPTH`), so recursion inside one expression is bounded by the language; only def-to-def demand needs the work stack.
3. **The memo is the engine's own, not salsa.** salsa 0.28.5 (2026-09-24) takes `hashbrown` and `rustc-hash` as normal dependencies, both banned by name in R-37, plus `parking_lot`, `crossbeam-*`, `boxcar` and others under R-52's allow-list, and it runs queries on the Rust call stack (crates.io dependency list). Its ideas carry over: revisions, verification by read set, early cut-off (backdating), durability. rust-analyzer's own post puts durability's saving at about 300 ms per edit.
4. **The memo key is (item, key values, trimmed context), with contexts interned as layers.** Pins, scenarios, members, paths and projections are layers that a cell joins only when its cone reaches them (ENGINE §3.2). A recurrence keyed by day or period is held as a dense column per (def, context), not as map entries. Estimated 60–300 bytes per cell against refcore's ~2.2 KB: X's 2.27 M cells fit in 0.14–0.7 GB (**inference**).
5. **The walk is event-driven, checkpointed and forked.** Days with no act cost nothing (PERF §1 says so); the monthly checkpoints of the amendment restart (PERF §4.2) also let `windows each` timelines, cuts and a member's partial walk share their common prefix (prior art: Excel's calc chain, Time Warp's checkpoints).
6. **Incrementality across amendments stays a seam; read sets are built now** (adjustments, `explain`, `inputs-of` need them). A cold run ≤ 1 s puts a turn within 1–2 s without salsa-style verification; the fitting step rides on pin layers the semantics already require.
7. **No eviction; release by scope** (a path, a `solve` step's pin layer, a projection): deterministic, unlike LRU, whose churn salsa's maintainers record (2025-01-22).
8. **Seam J first as** a member context with its own memo and a lazy walk up to the last day read; place-cone pruning second.

Decisions: D1-1 (evaluator form), D1-2 (memo: own, not salsa), D1-3 (memo key and layers), D1-4 (the walk), D1-5 (incrementality now or seam), D1-6 (eviction), D1-7 (seam J at first).

## 3. Findings

### Q1. Prior art, from source and authors' writing

**salsa and rust-analyzer** (phase 0's `datalog-incremental.md` §3.6–§3.7 covered the idea; what follows checks what changed and what it costs).
- **Version and dependencies.** salsa 0.28.5, 2026-09-24 (0.28.3 yanked); Apache-2.0 OR MIT. Normal dependencies: `boxcar`, `crossbeam-queue`, `crossbeam-utils`, `hashbrown ^0.17`, `hashlink`, `indexmap`, `intrusive-collections`, `parking_lot`, `portable-atomic`, `rustc-hash ^2`, `salsa-macro-rules`, `smallvec`, `thin-vec`, `tracing`, `typeid`; optional `rayon`, `shuttle`, `serde` and others (crates.io API, `salsa/0.28.5/dependencies`).
- **Algorithm.** One revision counter, raised on each input change; each memo stores its value and the queries it read with when each last changed. On a new revision a memo whose inputs are unchanged is returned; otherwise its dependencies are re-executed first, and if their outputs are equal the memo is **backdated**, treated as unchanged ("Even though the inputs changed, the output didn't"). **Durability:** "For each durability, we track the revision in which some input with that durability changed", so a query that read only high-durability inputs skips verification of each edge (salsa book, "The red-green algorithm").
- **Cost of verification, measured.** rust-analyzer marks the standard library durable and the project volatile; without it "any change to `src/lib.rs` necessitates checking all the queries related to standard library (which adds up to about 300ms)" (rust-analyzer blog, "Durable incrementality", 2023-07-24). A cache hit is not free: verification walks the read set.
- **Memory per memo.** salsa asserts at compile time that a `MemoHeader` is 4 machine words and a `Memo` 5 ("Memos are stored a lot, make sure their size doesn't randomly increase"), so 40 bytes before the value, the key and the out-of-line dependency list (`src/function/memo.rs`).
- **rust-analyzer's memory.** Moving to new salsa cost memory, cut to "only a 250 megabyte regression"; shrinking one per-memo sync state from 16 to 2 bytes saved hundreds of MB (salsa meeting notes, 2025-01-22). Release notes of 2026-01-12 (seen only in search results): GC instead of salsa interning for solver types "saves 648 MB and 31 s on self". Per-cell overhead and interning dominate a memoising engine's memory.
- **LRU.** Opt-in per query; an evicted memo keeps its dependency information "so that it can still compute whether values may have changed" (salsa book, "LRU"). The maintainers record that "LRU evicting within query computations can result in frequent (re-)evictions" and proposed manual and per-revision flavours (hackmd, 2025-01-22).
- **Stack.** A tracked function calls another directly, so demand chains use the Rust call stack; salsa detects cycles and panics by default or iterates a declared fixpoint (`cycle_fn`, `cycle_initial`; salsa book, "Cycles", docs.rs 0.26.1 source). rust-analyzer's stack overflows (issues #13006, #13655, PR #22740 on parser depth) are in its own recursive code. That salsa adds no explicit stack is **inference** from its API.

**Datalog and dataflow in Rust.**
- **Differential dataflow** 0.25.1 (2026-07-15). Computations are Rust code built into the program and compiled to timely dataflow; inputs change at run time. README figures (10 M nodes, 50 M edges): load 15.6 s, one update 228 µs, batches of 100 000 rounds about 0.5 s on one worker. It keeps indexed state per logical time in memory (`datalog-incremental.md` §5) (GitHub README).
- **DDlog** "is compiled into a Rust library", and "changes to the relational schema or rules require re-compilation"; it holds everything in memory; the repository is archived, read-only (GitHub, `vmware-archive/differential-datalog`). PERFORMANCE §1 point 2's "DDlog's mistake": a rule change would mean a Rust rebuild, against N-104 and the one-second law gate (N-2007). Tally's rules are data an LLM edits each turn; that settles it.
- **ascent** 0.8.1 (2026-08-29): "a logic programming language (similar to Datalog) embedded in Rust via macros", stratified negation and aggregation, lattices, parallel through rayon; no incremental evaluation, no rules loaded at run time (GitHub README). It shares DDlog's problem: rules fixed at compile time.

**Spreadsheet engines.**
- **Excel**: a dependency tree, a calculation chain, and dirty marking of "all the cells that depend on that new data"; a formula that meets an uncalculated cell is "moved down the chain" with its dependents, so the chain learns the order; independent sections run on several threads (Microsoft Learn, "Excel Recalculation").
- **IronCalc** (`ironcalc_base` 0.9.1, 2026-10-21) does a **full recalculation**: `evaluate()` surveys every formula and runs passes until one completes without restart; no dirty tracking. Evaluation is recursive (`evaluate_cell` → `compute_formula` → `evaluate_node_in_context` → `evaluate_cell`), with `CellState::{Evaluating, Evaluated}` giving `#CIRC!`. Depth is bounded by `DEFAULT_MAX_DEPTH = 64` **and** a measured stack budget `DEFAULT_MAX_STACK = 256 * 1024`; past either, the cell stays on an explicit stack and the outermost formula **replays** the stacked cells: "Every cell is pushed once in a pass, so this ends." Design goal: "Nothing here depends on the order in which cells are visited, only the amount of work does" (`base/src/evaluation.rs`, main branch).

**Policy evaluator.** **cedar** (`cedar-policy-core` 4.14.0, 2026-10-17) is a recursive tree-walk: `interpret` → `partial_interpret_internal`, a `match` on `expr.expr_kind()`, with partial evaluation behind a feature. Depth guard: `stacker::remaining_stack()` against `REQUIRED_STACK_SPACE = 1024 * 100`, assuming enough space when it can't measure; no memo of entity lookups visible (`cedar-policy-core/src/evaluator.rs`). Cedar evaluates one small policy set per request; it needs no memo, and Tally does.

**The evaluator's form, measured.**
- Neil Mitchell (Rust, a toy loop with no real operations, "a worst case"): AST interpretation 2.1 s, closure compilation 1.4 s, a fixed-size instruction stream 1.5 s, byte-encoded 1.5 s, hand-written Rust 0.003 s. "Interpretation by closure is a lot faster than I expected"; closures allow local specialisation at no cost (blog, 2020-04-28).
- Larose, Kaleba, Burchell, Marr, "AST vs. Bytecode: Interpreters in the Age of Meta-Compilation" (OOPSLA 2023): four pairs of AST and bytecode interpreters; with JIT on par, and interpreter-only AST "on par with, or even slightly faster than" bytecode; bytecode more compact for large programs (stefan-marr.de, paper page and abstract). Their interpreters are meta-compiled (RPython, Truffle), so the transfer to a plain Rust interpreter is **inference**.
- So the spread between forms is about 1.5× on a node-bound worst case. Tally's nodes are exact-number operations (a rational add is two multiplies, an add and a gcd, A3 F-3), so node dispatch is a smaller share than in a toy loop (**inference**).

### Q4. Memory

- **refcore's figure:** X held 2.27 M cells at 4.95 GB on the fast layer, about 2.2 KB per cell all in (shape.md; `4.95e9 / 2.27e6 = 2 181`). Python's object overhead inflates it; the ratio carries over, not the GB (shape.md point 6).
- **An engine cell (inference, no measurement):** key = item id (4 B) + key values (inline, days and periods as small integers, about 8–16 B) + context id (4 B); value = a `Num` on the i64 tier (16 B for a reduced fraction) or a pointer to the big tier, a hole or refusal by pointer; the register by shared pointer; the read set, about 3–10 reads of 8–16 B each. With `BTreeMap` node overhead: **60–100 B without read sets, 150–300 B with them.** salsa's own floor is 40 B per memo before key, value and edges (Q1).
- **Fit to the cap:** 2.27 M cells are 0.14–0.68 GB; half of 8 GB holds 13–67 M cells (`python3 -I -c`, 60–300 B). The largest load rows (B-07: 83 GB in refcore after 4 635 s; B-12 at 1 path: 18 GB) are refcore's figures: divided by the 7–35× ratio they land at 2–12 GB, so B-07 is the one that needs scope release (its 4 001 `solve` steps each pin a layer, which can be dropped after the step) (**inference**; benchmarks.md rows 4–5).
- **Eviction.** Not needed at these sizes if layers are released by scope. Scope release is deterministic: what is dropped depends only on the ask's structure. LRU depends on access order, which under 6 workers isn't fixed; results stay equal (cells are pure), but peak memory, and so a hit on the 8 GB cap, could differ between runs. salsa's LRU churn note (Q1) argues the same way.

### Q5. Incrementality now, or a seam

- **What the budgets need** (PERF §8, stale for 2.x but still the targets): a cold 33-year run ≤ 1 s; a warm run ≤ 50 ms (memo hits); a conversation turn 1–2 s; a fitting step ≤ 20 ms for two years.
  - If the cold run meets ≤ 1 s, a turn (re-parse, reload, reality run, scenario run, diff) fits 1–2 s **without** cross-amendment verification: the reality hash caches whole results (N-2004), and a scenario shares the base's memo in the same run (L §6.4's cost line).
  - The fitting step pins one input; a pin layer over the unpinned memo shares every cell outside the pin's cone (PERF §3, §4.4), and the walk restarts from the checkpoint before the pin's first day. That is pins and checkpoints, which the semantics need anyway, not revisions.
  - The warm run is a memo-hits run in one session: a memo kept across asks of one reality, not across amendments.
- **What incrementality across amendments adds:** revisions per row, verification of read sets on the next turn, backdating, durability tiers. Its cost: a revision stamp per cell, a verification pass, and the "read sets on a cold run" overhead PERF §10 leaves open (Soufflé's provenance 1.27×). rust-analyzer's 300 ms (Q1) shows verification is not free at scale.
- **Recommendation:** build per-run memo **with read sets** (needed for adjustments, E §5 step 2; `explain`, `inputs-of`, `readers-of`, E §3.2) and checkpoints now; leave revisions, verification and durability as a seam: the memo's cell type has room for a revision stamp, and the item graph's static cone is already built. Build it only if a measured turn misses 1–2 s. Gate 12 then requires every output byte-identical with and without it.

### Q6. Fit with the rulebook

- **salsa under phase 1's rules:** `hashbrown` and `rustc-hash`, normal dependencies, are banned by name (R-37); R-52 needs ~15 crates allow-listed; `parking_lot` and `crossbeam` bring locking outside R-42's one thread module; its macro output sits in clippy's blind spots (NOTE §1.6; **inference** for salsa). Tally's context dimensions would each be an interned argument (**inference**). Verdict: the engine's own memo, about the size of refcore's (`evaluate.py`, memo with `def_axes` trimming).
- **Recursion or a work stack.** BUILDER §4 asks for `enum Task { Demand(Cell), Finish(Cell) }` on a `Vec`. The prior art splits the problem:
  - *Inside one expression* recursion is bounded by the language: `LOAD-DEPTH` caps nesting at 8, and chains are flat (BUILDER §3). A recursive `eval(node)` over a resolved tree is safe and readable, like cedar's `interpret` minus `stacker` (R-16 bans measuring the stack).
  - *Across cells* (def → def demand, computed keys, aggregates over many keys) depth is unbounded. Two shapes: (a) BUILDER's pure work stack, where a body that meets an uncomputed cell suspends; Rust has no cheap suspension, so the body is re-run after the dependency is done (re-run cost; an aggregate over n uncomputed keys can turn quadratic unless its domain is demanded first); (b) IronCalc's hybrid: recurse to a **counted** depth (IronCalc uses 64), and past it push the cell on an explicit stack and replay. With the stack measurement removed (R-16), (b) is deterministic, since the depth is counted. Recurrences are filled iteratively in key order in both (E §3.2).
  - Recommendation: (b), counted-depth recursion with replay, plus prefetching an aggregate's domain before a replay. It keeps the common path readable (a def reads a def by a function call) and bounds the stack by a count (D1-1).
- **Maps:** R-36 allows `BTreeMap` or an insertion-ordered map with a reason; R-37 bans hash look-alikes. A memo is never iterated for output, but the rule is the rule: `BTreeMap` for sparse cells, `Vec` columns (read with `.get`, R-5's no-indexing) for dense keys, interned ids (`ItemId`, `CtxId`) as plain integers (BUILDER §3 already uses `ItemId(usize)`).

## 4. What this means for Tally's engine, in plain words

- **The engine is a spreadsheet with a calendar.** Each def at its keys is a cell, computed when asked for and cached (a memoised function's `Map`). The day walk is an `Array.reduce` over the days on which something happens.
- **Speed comes from not doing work.** refcore walks every day, and walks the same 15 years 64 times. The engine jumps from event to event and, when several what-if timelines start alike, walks the common start once and copies the state (a save game). That is a 10× kind of saving; a faster evaluator is a 1.5× kind.
- **The evaluator reads the tree directly:** names resolved to numbers at load, then a `switch` over node types. A Rust `enum` is a TypeScript tagged union the compiler makes you handle in full. No bytecode, no code generation.
- **No salsa.** salsa is rust-analyzer's caching library (a crate is an npm package). Its ideas are right; it pulls in two hash-map crates the rulebook bans, and it doesn't know Tally's contexts (cut, pins, scenario, member, path). The engine's own cache is a few hundred lines.
- **Recursion bounded by a counter.** A def reading a def is a function call up to a counted depth; past it the work is parked on a list and resumed, so a 12 000-day chain can't overflow the stack (Rust's "Maximum call stack size exceeded").
- **Memory:** an estimated 60–300 bytes per cell against Python's ~2 200; caches are dropped when their question ends, never by "least recently used", so memory is the same on every run.
- **Change costs what it touches, later.** Each cell records what it read (needed anyway for `explain` and back-pay adjustments); re-checking those records after an edit, as rust-analyzer does, waits until a measured turn is too slow.

## 5. Decisions for the user

**D1-1. The evaluator's form.** *Problem:* how a rule body is executed; it decides readability and node speed (12–37 M nodes per heavy ask).
- (a) Tree-walk over a resolved tree; one `match` per node type. (b) Closures compiled once per rule at load. (c) Bytecode and a virtual machine.
- **Pick (a)**, with counted-depth recursion across cells and replay past the count (Q6), and closures kept as a later, local optimisation for the hottest node types if E1-shaped asks miss budget.
- Cost: (a) least effort, most readable, about 1.5× slower per node than (b) on a worst case (Mitchell 2020); (b) boxed closures are harder to read and debug, and each needs the same refusal plumbing; (c) most effort, no measured gain over (b). Risk of (a): E1-shaped asks, where every day has work, are node-bound.

**D1-2. The memo: the engine's own, or salsa.** *Problem:* the cache is the engine's core; a crate is a rulebook decision (R-52).
- (a) Own memo. (b) salsa 0.28.5 with R-37 relaxed and ~15 crates allow-listed.
- **Pick (a).** Cost: a few hundred lines of plain code, written once; full control of the key and the layers. (b) breaks R-37, brings locking and macros, and still needs Tally's contexts as interned keys.

**D1-3. The memo's key and layers.** *Problem:* the number of distinct cells is the cost (half of all reads are misses).
- **Pick:** key = (item, key values, context id); the context is interned and trimmed per item from the static graph (E §3.2); scenarios, pin sets (by values), axis combinations, members, paths and projections are layers; a recurrence keyed by a time index is a dense column per (def, context); walk-bound cells live only at their walk point; input reads have their own memo by (input, keys, law-date class, cut class).
- Cost: moderate (the trimming analysis is load-time graph work refcore partly does already); readable as one table; the biggest memory and miss saving. Risk: a trimming bug shares a cell that shouldn't be; the cross-check against refcore (gate 10) catches it byte for byte.

**D1-4. The walk.** *Problem:* refcore spends most of its time stepping empty days and redoing the same walk.
- (a) Step every day, as refcore. (b) Event-driven only. (c) Event-driven, with monthly checkpoints, and forks of timelines at their first divergence day.
- **Pick (c), in two steps:** the event agenda and checkpoints in the first walk milestone (checkpoints are PERF §4.2's anyway); forking after the walk is byte-identical to refcore. Cost: the agenda needs an exact list of what makes a day non-empty (§7, spec observation 1); forking needs each timeline's divergence day from the plan pass. Speed: the largest single saving shape.md shows.

**D1-5. Incrementality across amendments.** *Problem:* PERF §4 designs salsa-style verification; is it built now?
- (a) Build revisions, verification, backdating and durability now. (b) Per-run memo with read sets and checkpoints now; verification as a seam.
- **Pick (b).** Cost: (a) adds a revision stamp per cell, a verification pass and a second code path that must stay byte-identical; (b) defers it until a measured turn misses 1–2 s. Risk of (b): if the cold run misses 1 s at the 10× size, the turn budget needs (a).

**D1-6. Eviction.** *Problem:* whether the memo needs a size limit under the 8 GB cap.
- (a) No eviction; release layers by scope. (b) LRU per item, as salsa.
- **Pick (a).** Deterministic peak memory; B-07's `solve` steps release their pin layers. Revisit only if a load row passes the cap.

**D1-7. What of seam J is built first.** *Problem:* refcore pays a member's whole walk to read one row (73.1 s; BUILD-NOTES J).
- (a) Member as its own context with its own memo; its walk lazy, run only up to the last day read and resumed from its checkpoints. (b) (a) plus pruning the member's walk to the cone of the places read.
- **Pick (a) first, (b) second.** Cost: (a) is the context and checkpoint machinery of D1-3 and D1-4 reused; (b) needs a place-cone analysis through decisions and strata (a decision reading place y keeps y's movers in the cone), which is the harder half. Nesting (a member that is a joint model) falls out of (a).

## 6. Unknowns until the engine runs

- Per-node cost of the tree-walk with exact arithmetic on the i64 tier (shape.md assumes 10–50 ns); whether E1-shaped asks need closures.
- Bytes per memo cell with and without read sets; the read-set overhead on a cold run (PERF §10).
- The checkpoint interval (PERF §10): monthly is a guess; Time Warp's literature finds the best static interval hard to fix and adaptive ones up to 12 % better (secondary sources, see §8).
- How late A's and E2's timelines diverge, which sets the fork saving.
- Whether the cold 33-year run meets ≤ 1 s at 10×, which decides D1-5.
- The quadratic risk of replay on aggregates over uncomputed keys, and whether domain prefetch removes it.

## 7. Contradictions with phase 1 and spec observations

- **BUILDER §4 (letter) against D1-1.** BUILDER asks for "an explicit work stack, not Rust recursion". D1-1 recommends recursion to a **counted** depth with an explicit stack and replay past it. It keeps R-16 (counted, never measured) and SO-10's concern (no unbounded native depth), but not BUILDER's wording. The user decides; the pure work stack remains the fallback.
- **Spec observation 1 (not fixed).** ENGINE §4.2 says "For each day t from S to E"; PERF §1 says days with nothing on them cost nothing. No section lists what makes a day non-empty (readings, observations, legs, decision days, lifespan ends, parts lifespan checks, `known` dates that move the look-ahead tree). The engine must derive the list; a step-every-day walk kept as a test oracle checks it.
- **Spec observation 2.** PERF §3 trims the context by cut class; E §3.2 puts the projection (holder, t) in the context of every cell evaluated inside a projection's walk. Whether a cell whose cone never reaches the walk drops the projection component is not said; X's 95 % of misses suggest it matters.
